Inside your camera

Before a camera can be a measuring instrument, look at what it actually records: a grid of numbers, partly invented, rounded, sampled and noisy. Each step below shows one of these effects on your own photo. Everything runs in your browser: your photos never leave this device.

Load a photo (step 1) or try the test chart.
Showing: – Move over the photo to read pixels · drag a box to measure noise.

11 × 11 pixels around the cursor

Each square is one pixel: three numbers from 0 to 255, red, green and blue.

1Load a photo

Anything works. Best: a sheet of white paper next to something colourful, in good light — then the same scene in a dim corner.

2A photo is a grid of numbers

Move the mouse over the photo, or tap it. The magnifier shows the pixels and the numbers stored for the one in the middle.

Why it matters. Every measurement in Exercise 2 is arithmetic on these numbers. The file you saved is compressed (JPEG, ISO/IEC 10918-1): look at the ratio in step 1. JPEG throws away detail the eye does not miss — but a measurement might.

3Three colour channels — and a secret

Each colour channel is a grey image of its own. Show them one at a time — in Exercise 2 you choose the one to measure in.

On the sensor, every pixel sits under one colour filter: in each 2 × 2 block, one red, two green, one blue (the Bayer pattern). The two missing colours at every pixel are interpolated by the phone. Two thirds of the numbers in your photo were never measured.

Grey = 0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B (ITU-R BT.601): green counts most, because the eye — and the sensor — have most green.

4How many grey levels?

Your photo stores 8 bits per channel: 2⁸ = 256 levels. Take bits away and watch the grey ramp.

Bits per pixel 8 → 256 grey levels, one step = 1
Why it matters. A smooth ramp breaks into visible bands when the steps get coarse. The threshold you will set in Exercise 2 is the extreme case: 1 bit, part or background.

5Resolution: what is too fine to record

Keep only one pixel in n along each direction — a camera with fewer, bigger pixels. Look at the centre of the Siemens star.

Keep 1 pixel in 1
Why it matters. Detail finer than two pixels cannot be recorded. With pick it does not simply vanish: it comes back as false rings and stripes (aliasing, moiré) that look like real detail. Average blurs it instead — lost, but honest. That is why cameras put a slight blur, an optical low-pass filter, in front of the sensor. In Exercise 2 you will see that blur at the edge of every washer, and the pixel size becomes micrometres on the part.

Averaging beats noise — up to a point

Average blocks of N neighbouring pixels and measure the noise of the averages. Truly random noise falls as 1/√N.

your photo ideal 1/√N
Why it matters. Noise is the camera's repeatability at the level of one pixel. In Exercise 2 a washer's diameter comes from the area of hundreds of thousands of pixels, and its edge alone has more than a thousand — that averaging is why the measurement can be far finer than one pixel.

Under the hood — the same in Python

For the curious: the notebook does every step of this page on the test chart or on your photo, with plots. It runs on Google Colab — nothing to install (a free Google account is needed).

Open the notebook in Colab

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

img = np.asarray(Image.open("photo.jpg").convert("RGB")).astype(float)   # rows x columns x 3
print(img.shape, img[100, 200])            # the size, and one pixel: [R, G, B]

grey = img @ [0.299, 0.587, 0.114]         # ITU-R BT.601 weights
two_bits = np.floor(grey / 64) * 64 + 32   # 2 bits: only 4 grey levels left
every_4th = grey[::4, ::4]                 # keep 1 pixel in 4: aliasing

patch = grey[500:700, 800:1000]            # an area that should be uniform
print(f"mean {patch.mean():.1f}  noise {patch.std():.2f}  SNR {patch.mean() / patch.std():.0f}")
