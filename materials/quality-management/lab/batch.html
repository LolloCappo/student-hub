Accept or reject the batch

You are the customer's quality engineer. A box of M8 washers to ISO 7089 has arrived, and the class has measured it with the camera gauge. Is the batch good — and how sure can you be, given that your gauge is not perfect either?

1Load the measurements

The CSV files downloaded in Exercise 2 — your group's, or every group's for the whole class. Select several files at once to merge them.

2The specification

ISO 7089 plain washers, normal series, product grade A. The hole (d1) may only be larger than nominal; the outside (d2) only smaller.

3How good is the gauge?

Every washer was photographed several times. How much those repeated values disagree is the gauge's repeatability (ISO 5725). Add the uncertainty of the caliper that calibrated it, and you have the measurement uncertainty of each washer's value (GUM).

Default: a caliper with a maximum permissible error of ±0.02 mm (check yours, ISO 13385-1), taken as a rectangular distribution: u = 0.02 / √3 = 0.012 mm.

4The batch

One value per washer: the mean of its photos. Masters are left out — their values were set by the caliper.

washers per class tolerance limits LSL, USL normal model with the batch mean and s
A box of washers has no production order, so there are no rational subgroups and no control chart: what you compute is process performance, Pp and Ppk with the overall s (ISO 22514-2), not capability Cp, Cpk.

5Decide, washer by washer

Near a tolerance limit, the measurement uncertainty decides whether you can prove anything at all.

Who must prove what? ISO 14253-1 puts the burden of proof on whoever makes the claim. A supplier claiming conformity must show the value is inside the tolerance reduced by the guard band; a customer rejecting a part must show it is outside the tolerance widened by it. Washers in between can be neither accepted by the supplier nor rejected by the customer — that is the price of an imperfect gauge.

Your decision

Write it as the customer's quality engineer would: accept, reject, or accept with conditions — in one paragraph, using Ppk, the expected fraction out of tolerance, the decision counts and the gauge's own limits. Many customers require Ppk ≥ 1.33 from their suppliers. A formal lot-acceptance plan by sampling would follow ISO 3951-1 (inspection by variables); here you have measured the whole sample you were given.

Under the hood — the same analysis in Python

For the curious: the notebook does this analysis with pandas, step by step, on the example data or on your class's CSV files. It runs on Google Colab — nothing to install (a free Google account is needed).

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import norm

df = pd.read_csv("example-class.csv")
df = df[df.master.astype(str).str.lower() != "true"]                  # masters were set by the caliper
by = df.groupby(["group", "part"]).od_mm                              # one washer = group + part name

s_r = np.sqrt((by.var() * (by.count() - 1)).sum() / (by.count() - 1).sum())   # pooled repeatability
u   = np.sqrt(s_r**2 / by.count().median() + 0.012**2)                # + caliper; for a mean of n photos
x   = by.mean()                                                       # one value per washer

LSL, USL = 15.57, 16.00                                               # ISO 7089, M8, outer diameter
m, s = x.mean(), x.std()
print(f"6u/T = {6 * u / (USL - LSL):.0%}   Pp = {(USL - LSL) / (6 * s):.2f}   Ppk = {min(USL - m, m - LSL) / (3 * s):.2f}")
print(f"expected out of tolerance: {1e6 * (norm.cdf(LSL, m, s) + norm.sf(USL, m, s)):.0f} ppm")

g = 1.645 * u                                                         # ISO 14253-1: 95 % conformance probability
status = np.where((x >= LSL + g) & (x <= USL - g), "conforming",
         np.where((x < LSL - g) | (x > USL + g), "nonconforming", "cannot decide"))
print(pd.Series(status).value_counts())
